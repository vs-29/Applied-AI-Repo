Coral bleaching: a short explainer

What coral is

A coral reef is built by colonies of tiny animals called polyps. Each polyp is related to sea anemones and jellyfish, and secretes a skeleton of calcium carbonate beneath itself. Over thousands of years, these skeletons accumulate into the large structures we recognise as reefs. Although reefs cover well under one percent of the ocean floor, they support roughly a quarter of all known marine species, which is why they are often compared to rainforests.

The partnership that feeds a reef

Most reef-building corals live in partnership with microscopic algae called zooxanthellae, which live inside the coral's tissue. The algae photosynthesise and pass most of the sugars they make to their host. In return, the coral gives the algae a protected place to live and the carbon dioxide and nutrients they need. This exchange supplies the majority of a coral's energy. The algae are also responsible for most of the colour of a healthy reef: their pigments give corals shades of brown, green and gold.

What bleaching is

When corals are stressed, the partnership breaks down and the coral expels the algae from its tissue. Without them, the tissue becomes transparent and the white skeleton shows through, which is why the process is called bleaching. A bleached coral is not dead yet, but it has lost its main food supply and begins to starve. If conditions return to normal within a few weeks, the algae can recolonise the coral and it can recover. If the stress lasts too long, the coral dies and is gradually covered by seaweed.

Why it happens

The most common cause of mass bleaching is water that stays unusually warm for an extended period. Many corals already live close to the upper limit of the temperatures they tolerate, so a rise of only about one degree Celsius above the usual summer maximum, lasting several weeks, can be enough to trigger bleaching. Scientists track this heat build-up with a measure called degree heating weeks, which adds up how far and for how long temperatures exceed the normal summer peak.

Heat is not the only trigger. Very low tides that expose corals to air, sudden drops in salinity after heavy rain, pollution, and strong sunlight in very clear, calm water can all cause bleaching on a local scale. In practice, several of these stresses often combine.

Recovery and long-term effects

Even when bleached corals survive, they pay a price. They grow more slowly, reproduce less, and become more vulnerable to disease for months afterwards. A reef needs roughly a decade without major disturbance for its fastest-growing corals to recover, and much longer for slow-growing species. When severe bleaching events return every few years instead of every few decades, reefs do not have time to recover between them, and the overall amount of living coral declines.

What helps

Because the main driver is ocean warming, the most important long-term action is reducing greenhouse gas emissions. Locally, reefs recover better when other pressures are reduced: limiting overfishing of the grazing fish that keep seaweed under control, reducing runoff of sediment and fertiliser from land, and managing tourism and anchoring. Some research groups are also testing coral nurseries and the selective breeding of more heat-tolerant corals, though these approaches work only at a small scale so far.
